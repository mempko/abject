# Code of Conduct

**Version 1.0 — 2026-11-11.** Adapted from the [Debian Code of Conduct, version 1.0](https://www.debian.org/code_of_conduct); see [Attribution and licensing](#attribution-and-licensing).

## Who is welcome here

Abject is for anyone who wants to build software where communication belongs to the people using it. That means people of every background, every gender, every sexuality, every race and every creed.

It does not mean fascists.

**Fascists are not welcome in this project** — not as contributors, not as participants in its discussions, not as members of its community. This is a stated position of the project, not an aspiration, a preference or an oversight. **This project does not tolerate intolerance.**

That is not a contradiction. A community open to everything is eventually taken over by the people who are open to nothing, and then there is no open community left. Refusing fascists is how the rest of this welcome survives. Karl Popper put it plainly when he wrote that unlimited tolerance must lead to the disappearance of tolerance. So the limit is written down at the top of this document, where it can be read, rather than left to be discovered later.

The project's code is written down for the same reason: its contracts — preconditions, postconditions, invariants — are stated explicitly and never disabled ("correctness over performance"; see [README.md](README.md#design-by-contract)). This document is that same discipline applied to the people rather than to the software.

## The code

The six points below are Debian's, adapted where Debian-specific.

### 1. Be respectful

In a project of any size, inevitably there will be people with whom you may disagree, or find it difficult to cooperate. Accept that, but even so, remain respectful. Disagreement is no excuse for poor behaviour or personal attacks, and a community in which people feel threatened is not a healthy community.

This is where the welcome above lands. Arguing about design, about licensing, about how a message bus should route — that is disagreement, and it is welcome. Arguing that some people belong here less than others, because of their gender, their sexuality, their race, their creed, or because of who they are, is not disagreement and is not welcome.

### 2. Assume good faith

Contributors have many ways of reaching the common goal of a system whose communication belongs to its users, and those ways may differ from yours. Assume that other people are working towards this goal.

Note that many contributors are not native English speakers, or may have different cultural backgrounds.

### 3. Be collaborative

This is a large and complex project; there is always more to learn here. It's good to ask for help when you need it. Similarly, offers for help should be seen in the context of the shared goal of improving the project.

When you make something for the benefit of the project, be willing to explain to others how it works, so that they can build on your work to make it even better.

### 4. Try to be concise

Keep in mind that what you write once will be read by many people. Writing a short message means people can understand the conversation as efficiently as possible; when a long explanation is necessary, consider adding a summary.

Try to bring new arguments to a conversation so that each message adds something unique to the thread, keeping in mind that the rest of the thread still contains the other arguments that have already been made. Try to stay on topic, especially in discussions that are already fairly large.

### 5. Be open

Most of the ways of communicating here allow for public and private messages. Prefer public methods of communication for project-related messages, unless you are posting something sensitive.

This applies to messages asking for help too: a public support request is much more likely to get an answer, and it makes sure that any inadvertent mistakes made by people answering it will be more easily detected and corrected.

### 6. In case of problems

This code should be adhered to by everyone here, but people sometimes have a bad day, or are unaware of some of its guidance. When that happens you may reply to them and point out this code of conduct. Such messages may be in public or in private, whatever is most appropriate. However, regardless of whether the message is public, it should still adhere to the relevant parts of this code of conduct; in particular, it should not be abusive or disrespectful. Assume good faith: it is more likely that someone is unaware of their bad behaviour than that they intentionally try to degrade the quality of the discussion.

Serious or persistent offenders will be temporarily or permanently excluded from the project's channels and repositories. See [Reporting a problem](#reporting-a-problem) for where to send that and who answers.

The exclusion of fascists is not a lapse of good faith to be explained away in this process: there is no reading of that position that is compatible with this community, and none will be accommodated.

## Reporting a problem

The project has a single maintainer and no moderation team. There is no community address, no conduct committee and no rota. This section describes what actually exists today rather than a body that could be imagined; the thinness is stated plainly because pretending otherwise would be worse than the limit itself.

There are two routes, and they are the whole of it:

- **Privately, by email.** Write to **Maxim Khailo (@mempko), max@mempko.com** — the maintainer address in `package.json`. Use this when the report concerns someone's safety, something that is not yet public, or when you would rather not say it in the open.
- **In public, by issue.** Open an issue at **https://github.com/mempko/abject/issues**. Use this for conduct in a public channel: everyone can then see the report, the answer and the reasoning. Public is the default for anything already said in public, because the project's whole argument is that the record belongs to, and stays visible to, the people it concerns.

## Who is held to this code

Everyone, starting with whoever is doing the moderating. The maintainer is bound by this code exactly as a contributor or a passer-by is, and there is no exemption above it. An answer to a report that comes from the maintainer is still subject to the same rules, including the rule that it not be abusive or disrespectful. If the maintainer is the problem, the same two routes carry the complaint, and a report made in public is owed a public answer.

## What can actually be done

The project can close issues and pull requests, block accounts from its repositories, remove messages where the platform allows it, refuse contributions, and say publicly why. That is the whole of it — there is no ladder of formal warnings, no standing office and no appeal board, because none of those exist yet. What is promised is that a report gets an answer, that the reason is given, and that the action taken is the least that keeps this community open.

## Amendments

Changes to this code follow the same public route as changes to the software: open an issue at https://github.com/mempko/abject/issues describing the change and why. Anyone may propose one, and it will be discussed in the open.

The welcome statement at the top and the exclusion of fascists are not a section this process can quietly drop. A proposal to remove them is not an amendment to this document but a proposal for a different one, and it will be declined.

## Scope

This code applies to the project's own spaces: the repository and its issues, pull requests and reviews; the project's discussion channels; and any place where someone is speaking for the project.

[PHILOSOPHY.md](PHILOSOPHY.md) sets out the principles this project is built on and is worth reading alongside this document.
